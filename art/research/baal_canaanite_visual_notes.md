# Baal / Canaanite Visual Notes

These notes are for art direction only. They should not be treated as finished canon.

## Practical Takeaways

- "Baal worshippers" should read as local Canaanite/West Semitic people, not members of a fantasy cult uniform.
- A good visual baseline is ordinary and elite Levantine dress: tunics, wrapped cloaks, long robes, kilts or short skirts where appropriate, belts, close caps or cloth head coverings, pins/fibulae, simple jewelry, and rank-specific ceremonial clothing.
- Priestly/royal ritual dress can be more formal, but should still look like regional court/cult clothing, not medieval robes.
- Baal imagery can suggest storm, rain, mountain, vegetation, drought, kingship, and protection. Use these as environmental and symbolic pressures.
- Ritual scenes should foreground food and drink offerings, animal sacrifice remains, altars, stelae, open-air sanctuaries, household shrines, figurines, oil lamps, vessels, ash, wine, water, and inscribed objects.
- Treat spirits and the dead as remembered humans with names, kinship, rank, obligations, and grievance.

## Evidence Notes

- The Louvre Baal stela from Ras Shamra/Ugarit lists Baal, raised arm, thunderbolt, lance, branch, dagger, mace, kilt, Egyptianizing crown, king, mantle, mountain, and sea among its features. That is useful for symbolic vocabulary, but it is deity/king imagery, not direct proof of ordinary worshipper dress.
- The Met's essay on Canaanite gods describes a smaller king figure in priestly clothing on the Baal stela and frames Baal as warrior/storm/fertility deity.
- Greener's survey of Late Bronze Age Canaanite religion emphasizes temples, sacrifices, ceremonial feasting, food and drink offerings, local altars, stelae, open-air sanctuaries, and household shrines. That supports scenes with ordinary people and localized ritual, not only elite temple drama.
- Late Bronze/Iron Age dress evidence is fragmentary and regionally mixed. Use clothing cues carefully and avoid claiming one universal Baal-worship costume.
