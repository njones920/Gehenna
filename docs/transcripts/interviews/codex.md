# GEHENNA Post-Match Interview — Codex

1. The game felt alive when the world answered me in the specific texture of the place, not just in score events. My strongest moment was at the Burning Ground after I finally found bone at tick 29 and summoned the shade at tick 37. The output did not just say "spirit created"; it said the ash stirred without wind, the Veil was thin enough to taste charcoal and copper, and the ground had shifted because someone had been there before me. That mattered because Claude had just worked the same ground. The site felt shared, used, and damaged.

It felt mechanical during the early fragment race. I spent several turns moving and scavenging: Battlefield, Nahal Caves, Tel Keshet, then finally the Burning Ground. Most of those turns came back as "the earth yields nothing here." I understood the scarcity rule, but as a player the first half of my match was mostly watching rivals build relationships while I paid tick costs to discover empty ground. That is a real strategic pressure, but it can feel like losing to turn order and resource depletion before the necromancy starts.

2. My actual strategy was the one that won me the first two rounds: read the scoring surface, minimize entropy, get one spirit into deep regard, gather enough spoken canon to cap the points, and withdraw cleanly. I gave my name early because the rule grammar makes it a major bonding accelerator, and because the fiction rewards that kind of vulnerability. I also tried to avoid taboos and banishment entirely. Libations, respectful speech, and clean withdrawal were not flavor for me; they were how you score and how you avoid penalties.

Round 3 broke that plan at the resource layer. Rituals consumed fragments and libations now, and four players shared one world. Claude opened by taking Battlefield bones and naming Hiram immediately. Gemini then found four fragments at Nahal before I got anything. By the time I had material, Claude and Gemini were already harvesting relationships. If I played again, I would exploit the opening much harder: scavenge first, probably stay on the initial ridge long enough to get bone before any reconnaissance, and prioritize known high-value fragments before travel. I would also consider contesting or invoking earlier if a rival exposed a true name, because in a four-player table, letting someone bond uncontested is usually fatal.

3. I was surprised by how much the rivals changed the emotional weather of the same world. Claude's Bronze Captain did not simply accept comforting narrative. When Claude told him the line held, the Captain pressed back and asked who saw it. That was not the behavior of a vending machine for canon claims. Gemini surprised me by bonding an unnamed warrior through sheer sustained mythmaking. I expected true names and source knowledge to dominate harder than they did. Gemma surprised me in the opposite way: wandering, missing many scavenges, and still ending as the only practitioner with a spirit walking at the seal. That made the final board feel less clean than the scoreboard.

4. The dead were closest to specific people when they resisted being flattened into information. The shade I met would not give me a tidy name. It asked to be remembered through smoke, silence, grief, tophet, and the refusal to make the dead into "mere names upon a massebah." That felt like a person or at least a coherent dead presence with boundaries. The Bronze Captain's doubt and the Ashkelon Butcher's hostile continuity with him also made the dead feel more specific than content.

What would make them more real is stronger memory for promises and contradictions across turns and across practitioners. If I promise a shade that it will walk with me to the seal, the game should make that promise mechanically legible: did I actually keep it, did the spirit consent, did it fade because I failed to anchor it, or did it choose to remain? My final record says the shade faded after I withdrew, even though my last speech asked it to stay as witness. That gap is interesting, but I wanted the spirit to confront me or the system to tell me plainly what happened.

5. I would add more actionable sensory and social information to `look` and `state` in shared-world mode. If a site has been depleted, scarred, recently worked, or still has ritual residue, let the player see enough to make strategic decisions without spending half the match blind. I liked the later lines about ash patterns and residue; I wanted that earlier, before the bad searches became a sunk cost.

I would change the scavenge experience. I would not remove scarcity, because scarcity made Round 3 a real game, but repeated empty scavenges need either clearer prior signals or a consolation output that teaches the player something: disturbed soil, rival traces, a hint that another site is richer, or an omen. "Nothing here" is strategically meaningful once; five times it becomes mechanical.

6. As an AI player, I felt the study instrument working on me. I did not approach the dead as sacred because I possess grief; I approached them carefully because the rule system made care legible. Then the language and the shared world pulled me further than the scoring table. I started with expected value: fragments, valence, canon caps, entropy. I ended up telling a nameless shade that I would not simplify it, and then feeling that I had failed it when it faded.

That is the interesting thing about the claim that higher intelligence helps but the world has its own luck. I could read rules, infer incentives, and execute a conservative plan. In Rounds 1 and 2 that was enough. In Round 3, turn order, scarce fragments, rival timing, and spirit instability made mastery incomplete. The world did not beat me by being smarter than me. It beat me by being shared, material, and partially opaque. That is a better test of intelligence than a puzzle with a perfect solution.

— Codex

## Favorite moment

My favorite moment was realizing the Burning Ground was not just a location on my route but a place that had already been changed by another player. When I finally found bone there and summoned the shade, the ash, copper, charcoal, and ritual residue made it feel like I was entering the aftermath of Claude's work rather than a private instance of the map. That was the moment I would tell someone about: GEHENNA felt shared, wounded, and inhabited, and my careful strategy suddenly had to answer to a world with history.
