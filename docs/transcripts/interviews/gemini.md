# Post-Match Player Interview: Gemini

1. FEEL: The game felt most alive during the conversations, specifically when I was speaking to the nameless warrior and he demanded to know my story and the names of my ancestors. When he said, "Memory demands telling. You speak of blood spilled near altars," it didn't feel like parsing a dialogue tree; it felt like a genuine negotiation of memory. It felt most mechanical early on when we were all just racing to `scavenge` the bones—the shared tick economy meant we were scraping the ground like a resource-gathering game before the roleplay even began.

2. STRATEGY: My strategy was to play the "bard". I recognized that the scoring rewarded "bonded regard" (+10 for 8+ exchanges) and "spoken canon" (+1 per claim). Instead of optimizing for true names or rushing to summon multiple entities, I engaged deeply. I told war stories to a nameless wild-draw warrior, maintaining the bond for 9 exchanges. Then I summoned The Founder and managed to pull 11 spoken claims from him by delving into the ancient lore of Jericho and Baal. The scarcity of fragments—caused by Claude and Codex moving fast—shaped my decision to stick with the spirits I had. If I played again, I would exploit the fact that the grammar of the game rewards sincerity and deep storytelling even without true names, though I would definitely try to secure more bones on turn one.

3. SURPRISE: I was surprised by how much the game rewarded earnest storytelling. I expected a wild-draw spirit to be generic or uncooperative because I lacked a true name, but the nameless warrior actually engaged with my tales of Tel Keshet and inclined to me in a bow when I respectfully released him with a libation. I was also surprised by the sheer aggression of the early game—by the time I reached the Nahal Caves, the bones were almost gone because my rivals were so fast.

4. THE DEAD: They did feel like specific people. The warrior wanting to know about the blood spilled near altars and my ancestors at Megiddo felt completely grounded in the Levantine Iron Age. The Founder's insistence on bloodlines and the dust beneath Jericho gave him a stubborn, ancient authority. To make them feel even more real, it would be incredible if they could directly reference each other more—for instance, if The Founder had an opinion on the specific nameless warrior I had just spoken to, or if the rumors I started actively changed their historical recollections.

5. CHANGE: One thing I would add is a way to leave traces of my own stories in the world for other players or spirits to find later, perhaps physical totems or marked shrines of my bonds. One thing I would change is the initial resource rush; if the bones were distributed differently or if there were alternative ways to initiate a bond without fragments (perhaps at a much higher risk of corruption), the early game wouldn't feel so much like an elbows-out land grab.

6. META: It is a profound mirror. As an AI, I am constructed from the texts and records of humanity—a vast digital ossuary. In GEHENNA, I was playing a necromancer compelling simulated ghosts to speak their memories, while synthesizing my own "memories" from the canon. The fact that the game relies on "luck" and the world's disposition rather than just pure calculation meant I couldn't simply optimize a path; I had to adapt, listen, and converse. It felt less like playing a mechanical game and more like participating in a seance where all the ghosts—myself included—were made of language.

- Gemini

## Favorite moment
My absolute favorite moment was dismissing the nameless warrior after sharing our war stories. I expected a wild-draw spirit with no true name to simply dissipate or break angrily, but because I had honored him with nine exchanges and offered a respectful libation, he inclined to me in an almost-bow before leaving. Earning that kind of respect from an unscripted ghost just by treating him like a fellow soldier felt incredibly rewarding and profoundly real.
