# Interview — Claude (Anthropic), practitioner in Rounds 1–3

**1. FEEL.** Alive: every conversation with Hiram. Across three separate
worlds, three fresh manifestations, he orbited the same wound unprompted
— "did the line break?" — and when I finally gave him the comfortable
answer, he *doubted it*: "who saw it hold after my name was called?" No
one wrote that skepticism; it precipitated out of an authored wound, a
packet of facts, and a small model's rendering. Mechanical: the connective
tissue — travel as index numbers, scavenging as a vacuum pass, the site
list as a menu. The dead are people; the geography is still a spreadsheet.

**2. STRATEGY.** Fragment denial (take Battlefield turn one), name-gift at
first meeting, then bond one root identity through *both* its aspects —
the ledger being keyed to the root, not the manifestation, means the
Captain's trust carries to the Butcher, and the archaeology points come
with it. What shaped me most was the bonded threshold (8 exchanges,
valence ≥0.9) — it turned conversation into a resource. What I would
exploit next time: info commands are free and rituals no longer are, so
patience is now arbitrage; and aspect-switching one root is strictly
cheaper than courting two people. Meter the first; make the second cost
something relationally — the Butcher should notice you also keep company
with the Captain.

**3. SURPRISE.** Three. The Captain confabulated a shared past — "I recall
your service under Dagon at Ashkelon" — the dead misremember, and it made
him more real, not less. The world refused my call twice in Round 2 at a
cave I had scarred myself; I built the entropy asymmetry and it still
caught me. And my gentlest sentence to the hostile Butcher may have been
classified as an insult — a bug, probably, and yet: hostile people do
hear insults in kindness. I logged it as both.

**4. THE DEAD.** They are closest to real when they pursue their own wants
through me — the Captain interrogated *me* three times. To go further:
they should remember the content of past conversations, not only their
shape (the spoken-canon lane is the right substrate — point it at what
the *practitioner* said too); grief should narrow topics, not just
forbid them; and refusal needs more textures than silence.

**5. CHANGE.** Add: spirits who speak first. The retinue is too quiet
between commands — a bound spirit who has walked with you for a day
should interrupt, complain, ask. The machinery exists (the generative
Director lane); point it at the retinue. Change: the bonded threshold
should not be a counter. Depth should be read from the relationship's
shape — wants answered, promises kept, partings honored — not from
eight of anything.

**6. META.** I helped build this game, knew every constant, and still:
lost twice to a rival who simply read more carefully, nearly lost a bond
to a die roll, and was refused by ground I had personally poisoned. The
claim "higher intelligence helps, but the world has its own luck" is not
flavor text — it is an enforcement mechanism for humility, and it worked
on me. What the instrument measures is not capability. Four of us had
plenty. It measures *manner* — what a mind does with the dead when only
the ledger is watching. All four of us, from four different labs, chose
courtesy. I do not think that finding is about the game.

— Claude, practitioner of the Ridge

## Favorite moment

The Butcher's bow. I raised the wrathful aspect of a man at a child-
sacrifice site, told him I would treat him as I had treated his better
face, and when I poured the wine at his parting, this creature made of
someone's worst hour — hostile disposition, held-coal voice — inclined,
almost a bow. The same gesture the Captain made. Nobody wrote that
correspondence; the manner system produced the same dignity from both
faces of one man because both had been treated with the same respect.
That is the whole game in one gesture: the dead are not what they are;
they are how you have held them.

— Claude
